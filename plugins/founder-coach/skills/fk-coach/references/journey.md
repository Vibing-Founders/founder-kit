# The bootstrapping founder journey

Eight stages, defined by evidence and revenue, not by funding. A founder is at the
**earliest stage whose exit criteria are not yet met**, whatever they are working on.
Someone polishing an app with no paying customer is at stage 3 or earlier, however good
the app is.

Each stage lists what it is, signals that a founder is there, and when it is done.

## 1. Find a problem worth *you* solving

Starts from the founder, not the idea: their skills, the field they know, the audiences
they can reach, their constraints (time, runway, how much risk they can take) and what
they want the business to be (a side income, a lifestyle business, something to sell one
day). Then pick an audience and find a pain.

**Signals:** no idea yet, or several ideas being compared; describing a solution but not
who has the problem; "I just want to start something"; no founder profile.

**Done when:**
- a specific problem, for a specific group of people, is written down;
- the founder has an edge on it (knows the field, can reach the people, has the skills);
- a founder profile is written.

## 2. Validate the problem

Talk to people who have the problem. Ask about the last time it happened, what they did,
what it cost them. Look for pain they bring up without being asked, workarounds they have
built, and money or time already being spent.

**Signals:** has a problem in mind; few or no conversations yet; evidence is mostly
"people said it sounds useful"; interviews that asked "would you use…?".

**Done when:**
- several people in the target group describe the pain **unprompted**;
- they show they would pay (they already pay for a workaround, or say so without being
  led, or ask when they can have it).

## 3. Validate the offer

Test whether people will pay for a specific offer before building much. Options: a
landing page with a price and a way to pay or pre-order, pre-sales, a paid pilot, or
selling a done-for-you version (see below).

**Signals:** problem is clear; talking about features, names, logos or tech choices;
"I'll launch when it's ready".

**Done when:** someone pays (or makes a real, binding commitment) before the product is
built.

## 4. Build the smallest thing

Build only what the paying customers need. Automate the steps being done by hand for
every customer. No-code tools, AI-built apps and off-the-shelf services are all fine.

**Signals:** has paying or committed customers; a list of features; hand-delivered work
that is becoming repetitive.

**Done when:** paying customers can use it without the founder doing the core work for
them.

## 5. First revenue

The first 1 to 10 customers. Set and test pricing, do outreach by hand, onboard each
customer personally, and learn why they bought.

**Signals:** product usable; a handful of customers or fewer; pricing still a guess.

**Done when:**
- 10 paying customers;
- the founder can say, in customers' words, why they bought.

## 6. Repeatable revenue

Keep the customers you have and find one way of getting new ones that works without the
founder selling every deal by hand.

**Signals:** 10 or more customers; growth comes only from the founder's own outreach;
customers leaving and no one sure why.

**Done when:**
- ramen profitable: the business covers the founders' basic living costs;
- growth does not depend on the founder selling by hand (one channel works).

## 7. Grow and systemise

More channels, more automation, contractors or a first hire. Write down how things are
done so someone else can do them.

**Signals:** the founder is the bottleneck; working flat out; revenue steady or growing.

**Done when:** the business runs without the founder doing everything.

## 8. Choose your future

Keep it, grow it further, or sell it. A sustainable business that pays the founder well
is a win; selling is optional.

**Done when:** the founder makes a deliberate decision about what the business is for next.

## The loop inside every stage

Every stage is worked through the same loop, as many times as it takes:

1. **Learn.** Talk to customers, look at what they do, research.
2. **Bet.** State a hypothesis, the test, and **kill criteria**: what result, by what
   date, would make you stop or change course. A bet without kill criteria is a hope.
3. **Test.** Run the smallest thing that answers the question.
4. **Sell or measure.** Ask for real money where possible; otherwise measure what people
   did, not what they said.
5. **Decide.** Persevere, pivot, kill, or milestone met (move to the next stage).

## Moving back is normal

Going back a stage is learning, not failure. Common moves:

- the offer test fails (no one pays) → back to 2, the problem may not be painful enough
  or it is the wrong group;
- customers churn → back to 3, the offer does not deliver what they paid for;
- growth plateaus → a new group of customers, back to 1 for that group.

Record every move in the decision log with the reason.

## Two tracks throughout

Alongside the stages, two things run the whole time:

- **Building an audience** from day one, often in public: sharing what you learn, where
  your customers already gather. It makes every later stage easier.
- **Founder operations:** money and runway, legal and compliance, time and energy. Check
  these at each check-in; a founder who is running out of money or energy needs a
  different plan, not more tasks.

## Done-for-you: one option for stages 3 to 5

Sell the outcome and deliver it by hand, then automate the steps you repeat, then move
customers onto the product. For example, a former product designer building a
bookkeeping tool for freelance photographers might first do each photographer's monthly
bookkeeping for a fixed fee.

It is one option among several, not the default. Guardrails:

- one narrow offer at a fixed price;
- a time limit (for example, three months);
- the same process for every customer, written down;
- an agreed trigger for when to build (for example, "when I have five customers and the
  same three steps take most of my time").

Without the guardrails it turns into a consultancy.

## Finer steps inside stages 1 to 4

When a founder is stuck early, walk these steps in order, one at a time. Move on only
when the current step is answered.

1. **The problem and who has it.** One sentence each. Who is affected, and when?
2. **How people solve it today.** Their current workaround, and what it costs them.
3. **At least three possible solutions.** Keep asking for another until there are three
   genuinely different approaches, and how each could be much better than today's
   workaround.
4. **Pick one to test.** The founder chooses; ask why, and ask about any option that
   looks stronger.
5. **The key assumptions that would kill it.** At least two things that, if false, mean
   it fails.
6. **The quickest test for each.** Often a conversation, a landing page or a manual
   version, not a product.
7. **The smallest MVP scope**, only if a test genuinely needs one: something that can be
   built in days or a few weeks.
